## Three positions, cleanly separated

| Position | Are there moral facts? | What settles disagreement? | Representative |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Moral realism / objectivism** | Yes, independent of belief | Getting the facts right | Parfit, Nagel, Sidgwick |
| **Pragmatism** | Wrong question | Nothing outside the conversation; solidarity, not objectivity | Rorty, Dewey |
| **Constructivism** | They're built by a procedure | Whether the procedure was followed | Rawls, Scanlon, Korsgaard |

**Note on the word.** "Objectivism" here means moral realism. It is *not* Ayn Rand's Objectivism, which is a specific ethical egoism plus a metaphysics and a politics. Cowen means the first. Acemoglu answers the first.

## What Rorty actually argued

The article Cowen links is ["The Priority of Democracy to Philosophy"](https://thehangedman.com/teaching-files/pragmatism/rorty-priority.pdf) (1988). Its claim is deliberately deflationary:

- Liberal democracy **does not need** a philosophical foundation. It does not need a theory of human nature, of rights, or of moral truth.
- Attempts to provide one are not just unnecessary but harmful — they make the polity hostage to a philosophical argument that can be lost.
- What we have instead is a historically contingent set of practices and a widening circle of who counts as "us." Rorty calls this **solidarity, not objectivity**.
- Moral progress is real but it is not progress *toward* anything. It is getting better at including people and reducing cruelty.

The famous formulation from *Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity* (1989): a liberal is someone who thinks cruelty is the worst thing we do — and the ironist knows that this commitment has no grounding deeper than her own contingent formation.

## Where Acemoglu sits, and why Cowen pokes

His actual words: *"in the same way that many people would agree science makes gradual and sometimes uncertain progress toward some better understanding, I think morally, philosophically, we also have to have gradual uncertain evolution toward what we agree on."*

Read that carefully. He offers a **science analogy** — and the science analogy is exactly what a realist uses. Science converges because there is a world constraining it. If moral inquiry converges the same way, something is doing the constraining, and that something is an external standard.

Then Cowen asks directly what the standard is and he says: *"I don't have one."*

So there are two readings:

- **Rorty-compatible reading.** "Progress" just means "increasing agreement," full stop. No target. Consistent, but then "progress" is doing no work — you cannot say a society that agreed more about something terrible had progressed.
- **Quietly realist reading.** He does think some moral views are better, and the evolution is toward them, and he simply declines to name the standard. Honest about his uncertainty, but not a stable position — it is realism with the metaphysics unpaid for.

The tell is his next answer: he *does* name something after all — meaningful individual freedom, as the "only starting point that we need to have." That is a foundation, offered one exchange after denying he has one. It is thin, and he presents it instrumentally (freedom is required for human improvement), but it is doing foundational work.

**"Objectivism, in some sense, is too strict"** is his own gloss: he is not denying moral facts exist. He is denying we can ever have a *clear map* of them at any given time. That is an epistemic claim, not a metaphysical one — and epistemic humility about moral facts is fully compatible with believing they exist. Which is why the Rorty comparison doesn't quite land, and why Cowen keeps pushing.

## Is objectivism live? — the honest answer

Yes, and more so now than when Rorty was writing. Worth knowing:

- **Derek Parfit, *On What Matters*** (2011) — the most ambitious modern defense, arguing Kantian, contractualist and consequentialist ethics "climb the same mountain from different sides." If he's right, convergence is evidence *for* realism.
- **Companions in guilt arguments** — if you accept mathematical or logical facts as mind-independent, the standard arguments against moral facts apply to those too.
- **The evolutionary debunking challenge** (Sharon Street) — the strongest live attack: our moral intuitions were selected for fitness, not truth, so why think they track moral facts?
- **Quasi-realism** (Simon Blackburn) — you can earn the right to talk like a realist without the metaphysics. Arguably where Acemoglu actually is.

## From the book

**Rorty is not in it.** Neither is Sidgwick, neither is Parfit, and there is no chapter, section or passage on moral realism, moral progress, or the standing of ethical claims. The comparison is Cowen's, supplied live, and Acemoglu accepted the frame without having worked in it. That absence is the finding, and it is more interesting than a quotation would have been: **the exchange Cowen forces is one Acemoglu has not had in print.**

What the book has instead, in the place where a foundation would go, is an epistemic premise:

> *"Premise 1. Fallibility. Every belief, proposition, or piece of knowledge we hold could be wrong, even spectacularly wrong. This humility should be the starting point for any discussion of knowledge, given the tendency of human communities to hold beliefs and follow practices that later turn out to be only partially true, incorrect, or even downright misguided."*

The lineage he claims for it is Mill on the fallibility of ages, and **Peirce**: *"we cannot in any way reach perfect certitude or exactitude. We can never be absolutely sure of anything."* Peirce is the founder of the tradition Rorty belongs to — so Cowen's instinct that there is a pragmatist here was right, and he named the wrong pragmatist. Peirce is also, unlike Rorty, a convergence theorist: the whole point of Peircean fallibilism is that inquiry gets somewhere in the long run, which is exactly the science analogy Acemoglu reaches for and exactly the thing Rorty denied.

**And the book has a third premise that is the real answer to Cowen's question**, though it is about beliefs rather than about values:

> *"Premise 3. Gentle Epistemic Improvement. Incorrect beliefs can spread and may sometimes become dominant. Nevertheless, there is a sometimes weak tendency for incorrect beliefs to fade over time, often under the influence of what David Hume called a gentle force. This is especially the case when there is room for debate and open exchange of information, and when the cage of norms is not too suffocating… Incorrect beliefs either have logical flaws or contradict observed empirical patterns, and these contradictions open the door for sceptics to challenge the system."*

That is a substantive claim about *why* the drift is toward better rather than merely elsewhere: wrong beliefs are eventually embarrassed by logic or by the world. It is stated as a premise, not defended — he grants immediately that it is *"a slow, laborious, and hazardous process"* — and it is doing exactly the work Cowen is asking him to account for. **It also does not obviously extend to values**, which is where the argument on this page actually lives, and the book never asks whether it does.

**The instrumental move, confirmed.** Draft 1 read Acemoglu as offering individual freedom as a thin, instrumentally-justified foundation rather than as a moral fact. The book is unambiguous on this, and states it most clearly about property rights:

> *"This supports property rights not because of a notion of natural rights or some principle that one's holdings of goods and assets are sacred. Rather, they are instrumental, a vehicle for effective experimentation and knowledge building."*

Whether that dissolves Cowen's question or relocates it is the live issue. Nothing in the book explains why *"effective experimentation and knowledge building"* is the thing worth having, and Premise 4 simply asserts it: *"Knowledge is not an end in itself. It is an instrument for achieving society's diverse objectives."* Whose objectives, chosen how, is where the regress lands — one step further back than Cowen pushed him, and unaddressed on either side.

## Working notes

**The strongest thing on this page** is the observation that Acemoglu uses a science-convergence analogy and then denies having a standard, and that these do not sit together. Draft 1 flagged this as unestablished and wanted it checked against the book, on the grounds that he may well address it in print.

**Checked. He does not address it in print** — there is no treatment of moral realism, moral progress or the grounding of values anywhere in the book. So it is not a compression artifact of a spoken interview; the gap is real. What the book supplies instead is a Peircean fallibilism plus a stated premise that wrong beliefs tend to fade, both of which are about knowledge rather than about values and neither of which is defended. That makes the observation stronger than draft 1 assumed, and it also makes it less of a gotcha: he is not dodging a question he has answered elsewhere, he is working without a net in a place where his book never needed one.

**Rorty on "why believe what you believe."** Cowen's follow-up ("Then why believe in what you believe?") is the classic anti-pragmatist challenge, and Rorty had a well-known answer Acemoglu doesn't reach for: *because I was raised in this tradition and I am not ashamed of it.* Rorty thought demanding a deeper reason was the mistake. Acemoglu instead reaches for individual freedom as a starting point, which is a more conventional liberal move.

**Answered:** the book contains no engagement with Rorty — no mention at all — so the second branch is the one that obtains. Cowen supplied the comparison and Acemoglu accepted the frame without having worked in it. Written up above.

What is not in doubt is the positive finding: where a grounding argument would sit, the book puts fallibilism and an instrumental justification of rights.
