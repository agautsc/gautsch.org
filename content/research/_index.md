<div class="rh-transcript">
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">TYLER COWEN:</strong> Hello, everyone, and welcome back to <em>Conversations with Tyler</em>. Today I’m honored to be sitting here with Daron Acemoglu. He is, of course, Nobel laureate in economics, arguably the best-published and, by some measures, the best-cited economist in the world. Most importantly, he has a new book out, <em><a href="https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/815318/what-happened-to-liberal-democracy-by-daron-acemoglu/" rel="nofollow">What Happened to Liberal Democracy?</a></em> This is a major work. I think of it as an attempt to redefine and revitalize liberalism for our current times. Daron, welcome.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">DARON ACEMOGLU:</strong> Thank you, Tyler. It’s my pleasure, and thanks for that wonderful introduction, which gets to the heart of the matter. That’s exactly what my ambition is with the book. Whether it will succeed, that’s much more questionable.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> I’d like to start with an oblique question. What is your main objection to social contract theories and contractarianism?</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> That’s jumping into the book, but that’s part of how I try to think of an alternative to the current conceptualization of liberalism, which I believe relies too much on <a class="rh-mark" id="a01" href="/research/social-contract-theory-and-its-critics/" data-page="Social Contract Theory and Its Critics">social contract theories. In my mind, social contract theories try to circumvent a key question, which is that we need to build consensus around shared moral values and shared priorities. In their place, contractarian ideas put in some absolute truths that we are supposed to agree on philosophical grounds. That’s a too extreme a view or too extreme way of putting it, but I think it fits the Rousseau end of it.</a></p>
<p>Then the other ones are adjacent to it, not exactly as strict as the Rousseau end. The Rousseau’s general will, for example, overrides any kind of consensus that a community, for example, could have because the general will is defined to have precedence over any kinds of agreements or shared moral values or shared priorities. I think by doing that liberalism avoids asking some of the hard questions. For example, how to deal with intolerance or how to deal with different views than its own, especially those that challenge it.</p>
<p class="rh-turn" id="a02"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> Richard Rorty had a <a href="https://thehangedman.com/teaching-files/pragmatism/rorty-priority.pdf" rel="nofollow">consensus-based defense of liberal democracy</a>. Is your view the same as his, or are there some metrics along which it’s different? Are you more objectivist in some way?</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> I wouldn’t say it’s more objectivist. I think my commitment in my mind has always been that it’s impossible for us at any point in time not just to know what’s really the absolute truth, but also to have a clear map of what is the right stance. In the same way that I think many people would agree science makes gradual and sometimes uncertain progress toward some better understanding, I think morally, philosophically, we also have to have gradual uncertain evolution toward what we agree on. <a class="rh-mark" id="a03" href="/research/rorty-objectivism-and-moral-progress/" data-page="Rorty, Objectivism, and Moral Progress">Objectivism, in some sense, is too strict.</a></p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> Say we’re not evolving toward greater agreement.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> No, sometimes we go back.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> Today, it seems we’re not evolving toward greater agreement, right?</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> We’re not. Absolutely not. I think you can blame that on reactions to globalization, reactions to technology, better authoritarian challenges. This is the most uncomfortable part because I see myself firmly within the left liberal tradition. I think it’s partly because of the failure of left liberalism. In some sense, if you think of why did Rome fail or collapse, we can tell a story about the barbarians were really too strong. No, really, Rome collapsed because internally it had problems that opened up weaknesses against outside invaders as well as some inside problems.</p>
<p>It’s the same with left liberalism. I think it became quite influential throughout much of the Western world and some of the emerging economies as well over the last 70, 80 years. It did not use that establishment power just in the right way, creating its own weaknesses that made it much more vulnerable to outside attacks. That’s why I think we are now going back because we are at an interregnum period in which ideas are battling again. In some sense, it’s just like the early 19th century where you had all these very different ideas and they were trying to get a toehold in the imagination of people. I think we are going through a period like that, except that there are really many more confusing ideas out there.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> Say when we’re not evolving toward greater consensus, what’s the external standard you introduce to judge which are the correct ideas and which not? <a class="rh-mark" id="a04" href="/research/rorty-objectivism-and-moral-progress/" data-page="Rorty, Objectivism, and Moral Progress">Like Rorty, you need one, though.</a></p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> I don’t have one.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> Then why believe in what you believe?</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> Okay. I think what I believe is, first of all, not as an external standard—I don’t know whether external standard or internal standard is the right word—but that we have to defend some degree of individual freedom because everything starts from that, both in terms of our meaningful lives, but also any kind of improvement in the human condition requires individual initiative, and that individual initiative is impossible without some amount of freedom, a meaningful freedom, not just saying, “Okay, you have the freedom to pray, but you cannot express that idea with others or you cannot turn it into action.” Some meaningful freedom.</p>
<p>That is the only starting point that we need to have. <a class="rh-mark" id="a05" href="/research/working-class-liberalism-and-community-elbow-room/" data-page="Working-Class Liberalism and Community Elbow Room">Around that, we need to build things with some sort of consensus within society. That’s what I think liberalism has to recognize, that you have to enshrine those rights, but then give enough elbow room to people to form their own community-level agreements.</a></p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> You present some different values in the book. One is nondomination. Another is noninterference. There’s others. When they clash, what’s the metric you introduce to decide how to resolve that clash?</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> I wish I had a perfect answer for that. I don’t.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> You have an imperfect answer.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> I have an imperfect answer. I think noninterference by itself isn’t enough because, at least as read or as interpreted by some philosophers and economists, it is just about being left alone from higher authority or typically state-level authority, although it could be some other authorities as well. We do also need opportunities and some way of actualizing our intentions and our freedom. That’s what the idea of nondomination, which goes back to Roman Republican times, recognizes.</p>
<p>It is not, in my mind, an extremely interventionist philosophy. It doesn’t say the state knows it best or that the state should always have an opportunity to override individual or group-level decisions. It does emphasize that providing people some amount of protection against those who have much greater physical or other power relative to them is something that cannot be done at the individual level. It has to have a community-level component.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> That’s a good answer, but is it an answer to the question? <a class="rh-mark" id="a06" href="/research/henry-sidgwick/" data-page="Henry Sidgwick">If we’re Sidgwick, we might say, when values clash, we look to utility, or maybe it’d be cost-benefit analysis to decide which gets priority. You’re not willing to say that, so why not?</a> What’s wrong with that answer?</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> Let me start with what’s wrong with a general-will type answer—I’m simplifying it—but I would say in a general-will type of approach is that we have a book in our hand which says when values clash and when there’s something, here is a higher value and we’re going to follow that. Ultimately, even though he’s a true liberal, <a class="rh-mark" id="a07" href="/research/john-rawls/" data-page="John Rawls">John Rawls also goes into that direction.</a> Now, you might think utilitarianism avoids that because it says, “No, we don’t have a book. All we do is we turn everything to utils and then we compare those utils.”</p>
<p id="a08">Who decides how to compare, how to turn things into utils? When there are externalities that are important? There again needs to be a book. I don’t like that book because who decides what that book is and how can we be certain that book exists? That’s why once we have the building blocks of individual liberty, then I think we have to go back to society and seek consensus on what our temporary book should be.</p>
<p>## On the working class vs. the elite</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> There’s a lot of talk in your book about what you call working-class liberalism, but say today in America, what exactly is working class? Household income, it’s almost six figures. Percentage working in manufacturing, it’s what, 8 percent to 9 percent. Private sector unions are very small. Is this even a meaningful notion today, the working class?</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> It may or may not be. That’s why I say that social democracy, as it evolved in Scandinavia and then spread to Europe and to some extent in a modified version to the United States, isn’t the right actualization of liberal ideas today because it very strongly relies on unions and a particular kind of industrial working class. Today we don’t have an industrial working class. If by working class I meant industrial working class, obviously that would be almost a vacuous notion. But we have the <mark class="rh-hl">vast majority of people who live by earning their living in the labor market. That’s the working class.</mark></p>
<p>If you’re getting your income from the labor market mostly, not from capital, not from your parents, then in my definition you’re part of the working class. The working class is distinguished by having a very diverse set of skills. Not just one skill. It’s going to have the manual part. It’s going to have the trades. It’s going to have office jobs. It’s going to have entrepreneurship of some sort, but that’s what the unifying theme is. That’s of course not a very simple unifying theme. There’s going to be a lot of heterogeneity within the working class. <mark class="rh-hl">That’s why I emphasize in the working-class liberalism different communities that are going to have their own practices and values.</mark></p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> Say when MIT decides who in the economics department should get tenure, that’s a working-class decision?</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> Yes. It’s like the edge of the working class because the tenured professors are a bit weird.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> Tell me what’s wrong with the following view. There’s an educated elite in most countries. They decide who gets a Nobel Prize, who at MIT gets tenure, how to referee economics papers at top five journals. I think for the most part they know best. Other parts of the working class need to feel included enough not to bring the system down. But actual outcomes and policies, we do as much as possible want to be decided by the educated elite. Is that a correct view? Because that’s close to my view. Is it your view?</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> No, it’s not my view.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> Tell me your view.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> Okay. How many hours do we have? You very succinctly summarized what—I try to convey this in the book, but perhaps not clearly enough—but what I think brought liberalism’s crisis. In two words, liberalism crisis is related to the rise of a post-industrial economy in which what you just called the educated elite has become both much more powerful and more numerous, and a philosophical shift that gave more and more priority and power to the educated elite. Whether you approach that from the left liberal point of view or from more of a right liberal point of view, that’s problematic in my mind.</p>
<p><mark class="rh-hl">We cannot have true freedom and true flourishing of a population when there is a clear hierarchy, whether it is by education, it is by race, it is by income. Just empowering the elite in that way is not going to cut it.</mark></p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> But we want to cover up the role of the elite to some extent, right? <a class="rh-mark" id="a09" href="/research/the-straussian-view/" data-page="The Straussian View">The Straussian view.</a></p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> Okay, well, <a class="rh-mark" id="a10" href="/research/the-straussian-view/" data-page="The Straussian View">if you cover it up really well.</a> No. I think the nuance here is that I am also very committed to expertise, obviously, given where I am. One challenge, which I mentioned in passing, and perhaps I should have done more in the book on this, is how do you utilize and respect expertise without creating the wrong kind of technocracy that is insular and too self-confident and, frankly, looking down upon the rest? Unfortunately, that’s what we’ve created in the United States and Europe, and that’s what working-class liberalism has to avoid.</p>
<p>## On automation</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> Now, here’s a sentence in your book that I very much disagreed with. I almost thought you left out a word. Let me read it to you, and I’ll tell you why I disagree. <a class="rh-mark" id="a11" href="/research/acemoglu-and-restrepo-the-task-framework/" data-page="Acemoglu and Restrepo - The Task Framework">You wrote, “Automation is central to my account because it severs the link between mass production and shared prosperity.”</a> Now, I view automation as <a href="https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/01/ai-labor-markets-and-wages.html?" rel="nofollow">creating</a> the link between mass production and shared prosperity. There’s a lot of evidence that automation doesn’t lower the labor share. <a class="rh-mark" href="/research/acemoglu-and-restrepo-the-task-framework/" data-page="Acemoglu and Restrepo - The Task Framework">I know well your papers with Restrepo.</a> That’s about the composition of labor income, right? Not about the total labor share. There’s a <a href="https://cepr.org/publications/dp21700" rel="nofollow">new paper out by Barany, Patel, and Siegel</a> looking at France up through 2019. Automation doesn’t harm the labor share at all, so why be so negative on automation? I just think it’s great.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> There are two parts to this. The first is how to put automation in our pantheon of different types of technological changes. By the way, by just having this conversation, we have gone way beyond what most economists do, which is to give up the pretense that there is just one kind of technology, that technology has a uniform effect. We’re explicitly talking about automation because there are other kinds of technological changes as well. I think that’s very important. <mark class="rh-hl">I think blackboxing of technology is one of the least productive things that we’ve done as economists.</mark> By the way, I’m guilty of this for the first 15 years of my career too.</p>
<p>Second is what is the conceptual and then empirical effects of automation? Let me start with the second. I don’t know this new paper that you mentioned.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> There’s plenty of papers showing automation brings prosperity, right? You could say the whole history since the Industrial Revolution.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> No, those are macro things, and let’s come back to that. The evidence in terms of what automation does, at least at the firm or the sectoral level, is very clear. It does reduce the labor share. I <a href="https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pandp.20181020" rel="nofollow">have</a> <a href="https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.33.2.3" rel="nofollow">several</a> <a href="https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2022-10/Tasks%20Automation%20and%20the%20Rise%20in%20US%20Wage%20Inequality.pdf" rel="nofollow">papers</a> on this. There are another 20, 30 papers. This is one area where evidence and theory are very well aligned. When you look at how it does it, it just really coheres with the theory. What automation is, is that it takes tasks or activities that were previously performed by labor and it transforms them to capital, algorithm, machinery, or whatever, robots. That has a first-order impact on the labor share because fewer things are done by labor.</p>
<p id="a12">Now, that doesn’t mean labor is going to get unemployed. There could be enough demand from nonautomated tasks for labor, but that would never come back to increase the wage enough to restore the labor share to where it is. That’s exactly what the theory is. Then when you look at the data, and in some papers we’ve been able to do that at the sectoral level, but in some papers really look at it very micro about what tasks are disappearing, what kinds of workers’ wages are changing, what kind of employment is changing, it coheres very well with the theory.</p>
<p>Now, at the macro level, it’s more complicated because there are composition effects between like Walmart effects and things like that, so we can talk about those, but I think the evidence, at least the ones that I know, is very clear that automation will reduce the labor share. That doesn’t mean reduce the wage.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> Sure. Wages are very high in the United States.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> Wages are high.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> What are we worried about? Even the labor share, if you adjust for equity compensation, it’s gone from 62 to 60. That might be modestly unfortunate, but it’s hardly a tragedy from 62 to 60.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> Hold onto that thought because it’s a great question, Tyler, but I want to come back to the first part of your earlier question because that’s very important. What’s my view of automation? How should we view it? Let me first tell you my view and then we can talk about whether it’s justified or not. I am not, 100 percent not, against automation. Automation has been ongoing since at least the beginning of the Industrial Revolution. It enables us not to do some of the harder, more manual, more routine, more back-breaking tasks. That’s fantastic. If the only type of technological change out of that many diverse types of technologies, if the only thing that we do is automation, then that’s not going to be a firm basis for shared prosperity.</p>
<p>When you talk about the US economy has high wages—for example, wage growth was very high in the ’40s, ’50s, ’60s, ’70s—that’s not, in my mind, just because of automation. Automation was a contributing factor, but more because of other kinds of technologies that created new opportunities, new tasks where workers that were displaced from the more mundane things could go, so that combination.</p>
<p>What I am really worried about in the age of AI, and robots’ entry into the manufacturing sector illustrates this very well, <mark class="rh-hl">if you prioritize automation and don’t do enough of the new task creation, new opportunity creation for labor, that’s when the labor share decline is going to become more severe, and the wage growth is going to be not there, or in fact, it could be negative.</mark> The thing about automation is that you’re right, automation could increase the real wage, but it could even reduce it.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> Well, it could. How many cases are there, say since post-World War II—Western Europe, Japan, South Korea, United States—where automation has on the whole decreased real wages?</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> <mark class="rh-hl">On the whole, but again, define on the whole.</mark> If on the whole is at the macro level when you look at the time series, that’s not just automation because automation is coupled with lots of other things. For example, my work on this with Pascual wasn’t that great because we did not have fantastic measures of new task creation. David Autor with his co-authors has written a <a href="https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262547307/the-work-of-the-future/" rel="nofollow">more recent book</a> that’s with much better data. What he shows and what we also found is a lot of the wage growth in the post-war period in the United States is related to these new tasks. About 40 percent to 50 percent of what the US labor market supports right now in terms of human work is related to these new tasks.</p>
<p>Really it’s not correct to say, “We’ve had a lot of automation since World War II and look at wages,” but if we zero in in periods of rapid automation, and that’s what we do in the <a href="https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/705716" rel="nofollow">paper on robots</a> with Pascual Restrepo, you do see wage decline. The wages of people who used to be in especially blue-color heavy manual tasks that were the ones that robots of the 1990s and 2000s went after, like welding and painting and things like that, the wages of those workers both—</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> Sure. Some tasks.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> —in the aggregate and also in local labor markets went down quite a bit.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> That’s a funny definition of automation because the new tasks being created, so often they’re coming from machines, right—</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> They are, absolutely.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> —and from computers.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> Oh, 100 percent, but that’s why I’m, again, if perhaps we’ll go to this later, when I talk of AI, I don’t say, “AI is going to be automation.” No, AI is going to be hopefully both automation and new tasks and then creating the right balance. Again, once we open that black box of technology, which I uploaded enthusiastically five minutes ago, then we are also saying, well, there are different rates at which these different components of technologies are advancing. During some periods, and when we went so much into robots, that was one, automation was really going very rapidly and new task creation wasn’t going as rapidly.</p>
<p>By the way, that’s not just in the nature of technology. When Japanese and South Korean, and to some extent German car manufacturers, for example, were introducing robots, they also redesigned jobs to create many new tasks that American companies didn’t do. There’s a lot of choice in that endeavor. That’s why I think AI is so exciting and so dangerous.</p>
<p class="rh-turn" id="a13"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> You have a <a href="https://www.nber.org/papers/w35401" rel="nofollow">new paper with David Autor, Beirn, and Scott</a> where when the number of young people is relatively low, there’s induced innovation, and that raises per capita income, right? Does that not make you much more optimistic? Is it at variance with your papers with Restrepo? It’s a much more positive vision, right?</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> Yes, it is a more positive vision, absolutely, 100 percent. By the way, the precursor to that paper is also another paper I wrote with Pascual Restrepo. Both papers use the task-based framework, but that just shows exactly like I was trying to say in the task-based framework and in my thinking automation isn’t bad. Sometimes automation is a response to scarcities of certain types of factors, in which case it’s really very important for enabling economic growth.</p>
<p>By the way, I think the possibility that there will be shortages of labor in certain, especially manual tasks, is indeed, as you say, a possibility for optimism, but depends on how you deal with it also. In both papers, you also see the element of choice. You have to do the technology, and not every society does that technology in the same way. We could get that wrong. We can get too excited with certain currents in AI and miss some of those boats, or we can use AI just the right way to deal with our shortages while also creating opportunities for remaining workers.</p>
<p>I don’t think there is any law of nature or economics that all of these things are going to work perfectly, or there is just one path for them to work in one way and not in other ways.</p>
<p>## On free speech</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> At the beginning of your book, there’s an excellent quotation from Frederick Douglass. I read, “Slavery cannot tolerate free speech. Five years of its exercise would banish the auction block and break every chain in the South.”</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> I love that quote.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> For you, what are the limits of free speech, and how do you decide where they go?</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> I wish I could say that, for me, there should be no limits to free speech. Ultimately, that’s what I believe if you allow me to define free speech. If I just said that sentence, you would give me an immediate counter example, having read my book. You would say, “But you’re in favor of regulating social media. Isn’t that restrictions on free speech?” I actually don’t believe so. <mark class="rh-hl">I think I would like to be a free speech absolutist while also regulating social media.</mark> I can say more on this, but it looked like you had a question.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> <mark class="rh-hl">Children are a complicated case for any liberal theory,</mark> I would say, right?</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> Absolutely.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> Putting aside children, are you an absolutist on free speech?</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> No. Again, I am an absolutist on free speech as I define it, but no, I’m not going to say that social media should not be regulated.</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> For adults, what’s the limiting case where you think we should limit free speech?</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> In my opinion, again, we shouldn’t limit free speech, but we should still regulate social media. The way that I think about this is the following. We should let individuals say whatever they want. Once social media can build very complex algorithms that human nature could not have anticipated, and we are evolutionarily not very well prepared, and they can almost weaponize certain types of statements, I think that’s no longer free speech. The free speech is when Tyler says something. I never want to take your right away, but I want to regulate what other people take your statement and how they use it.</p>
<p>That’s why I think I am an absolutist when it comes to free speech because I never want to restrict Tyler’s right to say anything. You can come back to me and say, “No, you’re not an absolutist because you want to put some framework about how others, companies, by the way, the most powerful companies humanity has ever seen, how those companies can take Tyler’s speech and use it in their algorithms or in the way that they feed into your vulnerabilities.”</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> <mark class="rh-hl">Say book markets and the printing press, they weaponized Marx and Lenin for many decades. <p>## On what comes next</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">COWEN:</strong> Final question—but before, just your book again, <em>What Happened to Liberal Democracy?</em> by Daron Acemoglu, new out this year—what is it you want to learn about next?</p>
<p class="rh-turn"><strong class="rh-speaker">ACEMOGLU:</strong> I have several big questions that I’m exploring right now. One is what we just talked about, how can we make AI, not as a regulation, but just ground up, how can you make AI more pro-worker? I think that’s just my big passion. Second, all of these questions, and actually your first question, intersect with the following issue: Where do you need talent in society? Where do you need talent, education, expertise? Is it enough to have it at the very top with the elite, the best scientists, and then it will trickle down, or do you need it everywhere in society? What makes the most radical innovations work? I think we actually don’t know the answer to that question. I suspect that it’s not as top-down as sometimes the standard histories tell, so that’s the second thing.</p>
<p>Then the third, it’s both bridging this gap between political economy, which is half of my work and more than half of my passion, and all of these things. What’s the role of ideas? The materialist conception, which is both dominant in Marxism and all of the leftist social science and economics, leaves very little room for ideas. When you look at the real world, sometimes ideas from individuals, but also from groups, create very different dynamics. That’s the third thing that I’m passionate about.</p>
