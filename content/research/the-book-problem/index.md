## The metaphor, traced

It appears four times in about ninety seconds, and each appearance does different work:

1. **The general will is a book.** *"we have a book in our hand which says when values clash… here is a higher value and we're going to follow that."*
2. **Rawls has one too.** *"even though he's a true liberal, John Rawls also goes into that direction."*
3. **Utilitarianism claims not to have one** — *"'No, we don't have a book. All we do is we turn everything to utils and then we compare those utils.'"*
4. **But it does.** *"Who decides how to compare, how to turn things into utils? When there are externalities that are important? There again needs to be a book."*

## Why step 4 is the good move

This is the strongest argument Acemoglu makes in the philosophical half of the interview, and it is worth stating carefully because it generalizes.

Utilitarianism advertises itself as procedural rather than dogmatic: no list of sacred values, just a common currency and arithmetic. Acemoglu's reply is that **building the currency requires every contested judgment the currency was supposed to avoid**:

- How do you weigh one person's intense preference against many people's mild ones?
- Do you count preferences people would not endorse on reflection?
- How do you price a life, a language, a landscape?
- Whose welfare counts — future people, foreigners, animals?
- How do you handle effects on third parties?

Each answer is a value judgment made **before** the arithmetic starts. So the neutral-looking machine has a rulebook bolted inside it, just less visible than Rousseau's. **A hidden book is worse than an open one**, because it cannot be argued with.

Anyone who has watched a cost-benefit analysis get used in a policy fight recognizes this immediately. The number looks objective. The assumptions that produced it were where the politics went.

## What he proposes instead

> *"once we have the building blocks of individual liberty, then I think we have to go back to society and seek consensus on what our temporary book should be."*

Three properties:

- **Thin foundation.** One thing is not up for consensus — meaningful individual freedom.
- **Consensus-written.** Everything above the floor is settled by real agreement among real people.
- **Explicitly temporary.** The book is revisable. That is the whole point.

## The obvious objection

**He still has a book — his is just one line long.** "Individual freedom is prior" is a lexical priority rule of exactly the kind he objects to in Rawls. He would presumably answer that a one-line book leaves vastly more room than a long one, and that his is *instrumentally* justified (freedom is the precondition of improvement) rather than asserted as an absolute truth.

That is a real distinction. Whether it is a difference in kind or only in degree is, I think, the unresolved question of the whole first act of this interview. And Cowen never quite closes on it — he moves to the working class instead.
