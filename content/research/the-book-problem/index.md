## The metaphor, traced

It appears four times in about ninety seconds, and each appearance does different work:

1. **The general will is a book.** *"we have a book in our hand which says when values clash… here is a higher value and we're going to follow that."*
2. **Rawls has one too.** *"even though he's a true liberal, John Rawls also goes into that direction."*
3. **Utilitarianism claims not to have one** — *"'No, we don't have a book. All we do is we turn everything to utils and then we compare those utils.'"*
4. **But it does.** *"Who decides how to compare, how to turn things into utils? When there are externalities that are important? There again needs to be a book."*

## Why step 4 is the good move

This is the strongest argument Acemoglu makes in the philosophical half of the interview, and it is worth stating carefully because it generalizes.

Utilitarianism advertises itself as procedural rather than dogmatic: no list of sacred values, just a common currency and arithmetic. Acemoglu's reply is that **building the currency requires every contested judgment the currency was supposed to avoid**:

- How do you weigh one person's intense preference against many people's mild ones?
- Do you count preferences people would not endorse on reflection?
- How do you price a life, a language, a landscape?
- Whose welfare counts — future people, foreigners, animals?
- How do you handle effects on third parties?

Each answer is a value judgment made **before** the arithmetic starts. So the neutral-looking machine has a rulebook bolted inside it, just less visible than Rousseau's. **A hidden book is worse than an open one**, because it cannot be argued with.

Anyone who has watched a cost-benefit analysis get used in a policy fight recognizes this immediately. The number looks objective. The assumptions that produced it were where the politics went.

## What he proposes instead

> *"once we have the building blocks of individual liberty, then I think we have to go back to society and seek consensus on what our temporary book should be."*

Three properties:

- **Thin foundation.** One thing is not up for consensus — meaningful individual freedom.
- **Consensus-written.** Everything above the floor is settled by real agreement among real people.
- **Explicitly temporary.** The book is revisable. That is the whole point.

## The obvious objection

**He still has a book — his is just a short one.** "Individual freedom is prior" is a pre-committed ranking, arrived at by argument rather than by anyone's consent, which is the shape of thing he objects to in Rousseau. He would presumably answer that a one-line book leaves vastly more room than a long one, and that his is *instrumentally* justified (freedom is the precondition of improvement) rather than asserted as an absolute truth.

That is a real distinction. Whether it is a difference in kind or only in degree is, I think, the unresolved question of the whole first act of this interview. And Cowen never quite closes on it — he moves to the working class instead.


## From the book

**"The book" is not in the book.** The metaphor is a spoken coinage — it appears four times in ninety seconds of conversation and nowhere in twelve hours of text. What the book has instead is the same commitment stated flatly, twice, as the closing move of two different chapters:

> *"Liberalism is a framework, rather than a set of cast-in-stone policies and rules that can help us navigate these trade-offs. But ultimate decisions will have to be made by self-governing societies."* — Chapter 2, closing the fourth pillar

> *"How these elements can be fused together and the trade-offs navigated should not be viewed as being cast in stone. Liberalism, as I have already emphasized, should be humble and understood as a framework for supporting freedoms, building collective knowledge, and navigating various trade-offs, rather than as a precise set of rules that can be easily applied in all cases."* — Chapter 3

So "temporary book" is a fair gloss on his position. **"Written by consensus" is the part the book does more work on than the interview**: Chapter 3 supplies two cases of a contested value question being settled by argument among real people rather than by a rule — the Irish abortion referendum and the American path to same-sex marriage — which is what the interview asserts but never demonstrates.

**Where the temporariness comes from.** Not from modesty. Chapter 2 builds the whole framework from four premises, and the first is the load-bearing one:

> *"Premise 1. Fallibility. Every belief, proposition, or piece of knowledge we hold could be wrong, even spectacularly wrong. This humility should be the starting point for any discussion of knowledge."*

He runs it back to Mill on the fallibility of ages and to **Peirce's fallibilism** — *"we cannot in any way reach perfect certitude or exactitude"* — and forward through three more premises (social learning in community; gentle epistemic improvement; the desirability of collective knowledge) to four pillars: the vitality of experimentation, non-domination and self-government, economic growth, and soft communitarianism. The book is revisable because **every belief is revisable**. That is an epistemic argument, not a political preference, and it is the strongest available answer to "why should your book get to be temporary when Rousseau's wasn't."

**The utilitarianism move is interview-only — and that is worth knowing.** Step 4 above is the sharpest thing Acemoglu says in the philosophical half of the conversation. It has no counterpart in the book. *Utilitarian* and *utilitarianism* appear nowhere in it; Bentham and Mill show up only as classical liberals who defined liberty as non-interference, and are criticized on that basis, not on the arithmetic. He is answering Cowen's frame in real time, well, and off the page. **Which cuts both ways:** it is genuine live thinking rather than a rehearsed line, and it is also unbacked — the objection he presses on utils in ninety seconds is not one he has committed to print and had to defend.

**The one-line book, re-measured.** The interview makes the floor sound like a single item ("meaningful individual freedom"). Chapter 10 names it, and it is four:

> *"Liberal democracy must strive for maximum tolerance but should never accept community traditions and practices that suppress basic individual freedoms, including freedom of expression, freedom to acquire education, equality before the law, and the right to exit the community."*

So the crux below is not "one line versus a rulebook." It is **four lines versus a rulebook**, which is a weaker position than the interview implies. What the book adds in his defence is that these are justified instrumentally rather than asserted — Chapter 2 makes the point most clearly about property rights, which it supports *"not because of a notion of natural rights or some principle that one's holdings of goods and assets are sacred. Rather, they are instrumental, a vehicle for effective experimentation and knowledge building"* — and then immediately draws the consequence a rulebook could not: *"This justification does not suggest that property rights are inviolable."* A right that can lose is not an entry in a book of trumps. Whether the same is true of the four in Chapter 10, which are stated without any such escape clause, is the live question.

## Working notes
The utilitarianism critique here is genuinely good and not original to Acemoglu — it's close to Sen's argument about the informational basis of welfarism, and to Amartya Sen and Bernard Williams's *Utilitarianism and Beyond* (1982). Worth adding that lineage in draft 2 so it's clear he's joining an argument rather than inventing one. **The book check makes this more interesting, not less:** he isn't joining that argument in print at all, so the interview is where he does it.

**Unresolved and worth your judgment:** is a short book meaningfully different from a rulebook? Still unresolved on purpose — but the book moved the terms. The floor is four items, not one, and the strongest thing in its favour is not brevity but the instrumental justification and the explicit admission that such rights are not inviolable. Both sides are laid out above; the choice is still yours.
