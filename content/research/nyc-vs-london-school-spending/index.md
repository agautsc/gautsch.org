## New York City

| Year | Per-pupil spending | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 2021–22 | **$35,914** | Highest of the 100 largest US school systems |
| 2024 | **~$36,000+** | +21% in real terms over the prior decade |
| 2025–26 (projected) | **$42,168** | New national record |

New York *State* as a whole spends about **$30,723** per student — also first in the nation. The state's spending gap over the national average has **more than quadrupled since 2000**.

The enrolment fact is the one that makes the number strange: NYC has roughly **100,000 fewer students** than in 2019–20, and spending per student rose anyway. Fixed costs — pensions, debt service, buildings, transportation — do not fall when a child leaves.

**Sources:** [Citizens Budget Commission](https://cbcny.org/research/highest-costs-middling-marks) · [Empire Center](https://www.empirecenter.org/publications/ny-per-pupil-school-spending-led-all-us-by-record-margin-in-2021-22/) · [Reason Foundation](https://reason.org/commentary/new-york-public-school-spending-continues-to-grow-while-enrollment-declines/)

## The comparison, honestly

**Headline:** NYC ≈ $42,000 vs London ≈ $11,000 — about **3.8×**.

**Why that overstates the real gap:**

1. **Different things are being counted.** The US per-pupil figure is *total current expenditure*: teacher pensions and retiree health, debt service, student transportation, food service, special education, central administration. England's NFF number is the **mainstream schools block** — it excludes high-needs funding, most capital, and portions of pension costs funded through separate grants.
2. **Pensions and retiree health are enormous in NYC** and are a legacy-cost transfer, not instruction.
3. **Health care is in the number.** US teacher compensation carries employer health insurance; the UK's does not, because the NHS does.
4. **Cost of living and salary structure** differ; so does the definition of a "pupil."

**A fairer frame — national totals.** OECD comparisons of total primary-and-secondary spending per student, PPP-adjusted, put the **US around $15,000–16,000** and the **UK around $12,000–14,000** — a gap of maybe 15–25%, not 400%. NYC is an outlier *within* the US, not a representative American figure. ⚠ These OECD figures are from memory and should be re-sourced before use.

**What survives the caveats:** NYC really does spend far more per student than almost anywhere in the world, the gap has widened sharply since 2000, and spending has continued rising as enrolment falls. Cowen's point stands. The 3.8× number does not survive scrutiny; a 2–2.5× real-resources gap probably does.

## Why this matters in the conversation

This is the exchange where Acemoglu is at his most exposed and he knows it. His claim is that America has "given up on the education of about a third to a half of the youth." Cowen's counter is that NYC is the richest-funded school system on earth and the results are middling — so *money* is plainly not what was given up on.

Acemoglu concedes immediately — *"Oh, yes, I know… We're throwing money at it, but we're not really dealing with the root causes"* — and pivots to a claim about dysfunction and ideology, then agrees teachers' unions "need to be reconstituted." **That is a substantial retreat from "we have given up," and it happens in three lines.**

The interesting thing for you: the CBC's framing is literally *"Highest Costs, Middling Marks."* Both men accept the premise that the money isn't reaching the outcome. Neither offers a mechanism.
